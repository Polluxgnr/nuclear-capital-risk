\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Abstract}---Nuclear energy provides reliable, low-carbon baseload electricity, but new builds suffer from multi-year delays and severe Interest During Construction (IDC) compounding. Analyzing the August 2026 Global Nuclear Power Tracker (1,825 reactors), we find that \textbf{180.99\,GW across 198 operating reactors (44.38\% of global operating capacity)} has reached or exceeded its 40-year design lifetime. Historical construction lead times average \textbf{8.76 years} (median: 7.16 years), making delays the empirical baseline. We model project finance cash flows across Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) scenarios ($r \in [4\%, 10\%]$). We find that 20-year Long-Term Operation (LTO / life extension) at \$1,200/kW yields an ultra-stable Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE) of \textbf{\$40.47 to \$48.98/MWh}. In contrast, new-build Gen-III+ reactors (\$7,500/kW overnight) suffer dramatic debt compounding: at 7\% WACC with a 5-year delay, capex balloons to \$12,713/kW and LCOE to \$138.14/MWh. Under 10\% merchant WACC with a 7-year delay, Gen-III+ LCOE reaches \$204.60/MWh (4.6$\times$ LTO). We conclude that extending existing fleets is the most capital-efficient climate investment available.
\end{abstract}

% --- Section 1: Introduction ---
\section{Introduction}
% Explains why nuclear power is essential for decarbonization and highlights the financial bottlenecks.
Decarbonizing power grids requires firm, low-carbon generation to back up intermittent wind and solar resources \cite{ipcc2022mitigation}. While nuclear power emits negligible operational greenhouse gases, private investment in greenfield reactors is paralyzed by financial friction:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Capital Lumpiness}: A twin-unit gigawatt plant costs \$15B to \$25B.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Construction Delays}: Megaprojects regularly experience multi-year schedule slippages.
    \item \textbf{Compounding Cost of Capital}: Interest During Construction (IDC) accumulates exponentially during unproductive building years, exacerbated by post-2022 interest rate increases \cite{damodaran2026wacc}.
    \item \textbf{The Retirement Cliff}: Fleets built in the 1970s and 1980s are reaching the end of their original 40-year licenses.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Core Question:} \textit{Does long-term operation (LTO) of aging reactors offer a financially superior risk-adjusted return over new-build investments under rising cost-of-capital scenarios?}

\subsection{Empirical Construction Lead Times}
We compute historical lead times as $\Delta t = (\text{COD} - \text{Start})/365.25$. As shown in \cref{fig:lead_times}, completed reactors exhibit an empirical mean lead time of \textbf{8.76 years} and a median of \textbf{7.16 years} (IQR: 5.66 to 10.18 years). Pressurized Water Reactors (PWR) median is 7.14 years, whereas Boiling Water Reactors (BWR) median is 6.42 years. Multi-year delays are statistically standard in Western builds.

% --- Section 3: Financial Methodology ---
\section{Quantitative Financial Methodology}
% Presents the mathematical formulas used to model capital compounding and LCOE.
We calibrate three primary technology configurations based on IEA/NEA benchmarks \cite{iea2020projected}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gen-III+}: Overnight capex \$7,500/kW, nominal construction time $T_0 = 7.0$ years, lifetime $N = 60$ years.
    \item \textbf{SMR FOAK}: Overnight capex \$9,000/kW, nominal time $T_0 = 4.0$ years, lifetime $N = 40$ years.
    \item \textbf{20-Yr LTO}: Overnight capex \$1,200/kW, outage time $T_0 = 2.0$ years, extended lifetime $N = 20$ years.
\end{itemize}

% --- EQUATION 1: S-Curve Cumulative Spend Distribution ---
% Describes the cumulative capital disbursement over time using a continuous Beta distribution.
\subsection{S-Curve Capital Spend Profile}
Capital is spent according to an S-curve, modeled by the cumulative distribution function of a Beta distribution over normalized time $\tau = t / T \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
S(\tau) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)} \int_0^\tau u^{\alpha-1} (1-u)^{\beta-1} \, \mathrm{d}u
\label{eq:scurve}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = 2.5$ and $\beta = 2.5$. The annual spending weight is $w_t = S(t/T) - S((t-1)/T)$.

% --- EQUATION 2: Discrete IDC Compounding Equation ---
% Sums annual capital disbursements compounded forward to commercial operation.
\subsection{Interest During Construction (IDC)}
For a construction duration $T = T_0 + \Delta t$ under a Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) $r$, annual disbursements compound forward to commercial operation:
\begin{equation}
\text{Capex}_{\text{total}}(T, r) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} w_t \cdot \text{Capex}_{\text{esc}} \cdot (1 + r)^{T - t + 0.5}
\label{eq:idc}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Capex}_{\text{esc}} = \text{Capex}_{\text{overnight}} \cdot (1 + 0.015 \cdot \Delta t)$ includes a 1.5\% annual site carrying cost escalation during delays.

% --- EQUATION 3: LCOE and Capital Recovery Factor (CRF) ---
% Computes the Levelized Cost of Electricity in $/MWh.
\subsection{Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE)}
The Levelized Cost of Electricity balances discounted lifecycle capital and operating costs against total generation:
\begin{equation}
\text{LCOE} = \frac{\text{CRF}(r, N) \cdot \text{Capex}_{\text{total}} + \text{Fixed O\&M}}{8760 \cdot \text{CF}} + \text{VarCost}
\label{eq:lcoe}
\end{equation}
where $\text{CRF}(r, N) = \frac{r(1+r)^N}{(1+r)^N - 1}$ is the Capital Recovery Factor, $\text{CF} = 0.88$ is the capacity factor, and $\text{VarCost} = \text{Var O\&M} + \text{Fuel Cost}$.

\subsection{LCOE Superiority of Fleet Life Extension}
As presented in \cref{fig:lcoe_chart} and \cref{tab:lcoe_table}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{LTO Resilience}: LTO generation cost remains between **\$40.47 and \$48.98/MWh** across all WACCs, well below wholesale power benchmarks (\$60--\$90/MWh).
    \item \textbf{New-Build Penalty}: On-time Gen-III+ produces electricity at \$113.01/MWh at 7\% WACC (2.54$\times$ LTO). With a 5-year delay, generation costs rise to \$138.14/MWh (3.11$\times$ LTO).
    \item \textbf{SMR FOAK Economics}: SMRs produce power at \$128.65/MWh at 7\% WACC, failing to beat LTO economics prior to factory learning curves.
\end{enumerate}

% --- Section 5: Strategic Recommendations ---
\section{Strategic Recommendations}
% Summarizes actionable policy and utility management insights.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prioritize LTO First}: Extending the 181\,GW of mature reactors costs \$217 billion, saving \$1.1 trillion compared to equivalent greenfield replacement capacity.
    \item \textbf{De-Risk New Builds via Policy}: Governments must implement Regulated Asset Base (RAB) models or Contracts for Difference (CfD) to compress WACC below 5\%, eliminating fatal IDC debt compounding.
\end{enumerate}

% --- Section 6: Conclusion ---
\section{Conclusion}
Fleet life extension (LTO) offers the lowest-cost, lowest-risk climate finance option for low-carbon baseload electricity. Under rising interest rates and empirical construction delays, new builds suffer acute capital erosion. Extending the operational life of the 181\,GW aging fleet must be the immediate focus for utility capital allocation.
